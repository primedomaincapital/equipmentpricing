How Long Does Heavy Equipment Hold Its Value?

A new machine loses 20–30% of its value in the first year — then the curve flattens, until the hour meter catches up with it. What excavators, dozers, and loaders are actually worth at every age.

10 min read

On a Ritchie Bros. auction floor in the second quarter of 2026, the median crawler excavator that changed hands in Europe was eight years old. It sold for about £24,187, after a median 5,860 hours of work — down from 6,548 hours a year earlier, as auction volumes rose 14 percent. That machine is the heavy equipment market in miniature: eight years of digging, still worth real money, sold to a buyer who had read its hour meter more carefully than its birth certificate. The question that buyer answered, and that every owner eventually faces, is how much of a machine’s value survives each year of work — and when the surviving value stops being worth the wait.

Read the median for what it is. It is dominated by small machines — Caterpillar’s 306 mini excavator was the top-selling individual model in the quarter — so it describes the market’s center of gravity, not the price of a 20-ton machine. What it does show is the shape of the whole business: heavy equipment is not a wasting asset that falls to zero. It is a durable good with a long, slow slide, and the slide has a shape you can plan around.

The shape of the curve

Used equipment loses a roughly constant percentage of its remaining value each year, not a constant number of dollars. That declining-balance shape is the single most useful fact about residual value, because it explains why the first year hurts the most and why the tenth year barely registers. In the first year, a new machine typically loses 20 to 30 percent of its purchase value, according to market data from EquipmentWatch and Iron Solutions, and to Cleveland Brothers’ depreciation analysis. In years two and three it loses another 6 to 8 percent a year. In years four to seven the decline slows to 5 to 8 percent a year. From year eight on, the value is relatively stable — the machine is worth what its remaining working life is worth, and that changes slowly.

Put numbers on it. JumboBee’s 2026 worked example buys a new wheel loader for $180,000: after year one it is worth approximately $130,000, after year three approximately $105,000, and after year five approximately $85,000. The first year alone takes $50,000 — more than the next two years combined. That is the curve every buy-versus-rent decision in this market is really about, and it is a market-value curve, not a tax schedule: the depreciation a fleet claims on its books follows MACRS rules, and the two have almost nothing to do with each other.

Three machines, three curves

Not every machine slides at the same speed. Excavators lose roughly 12 to 15 percent a year, wheel loaders 10 to 13 percent, and dozers 10 to 12 percent, according to EquipBook’s 2026 depreciation guide — excavators fastest, because their technology dates quickest and their buyer pool is the most price-sensitive; dozers slowest, because large dozers live long lives in mining and land clearing, where buyers care about push power rather than model year. The five-year picture, measured at 5,000 hours against original list price, makes the gap concrete. EquipmentWatch residual-value data and Ritchie Bros. auction results from the first quarter of 2026, compiled by HeavyDutyYard, show a Caterpillar 320 mid-size excavator retaining 52 to 58 percent of its list price at 5,000 hours, against 46 to 52 percent for a comparable Komatsu PC210 and 45 to 51 percent for a John Deere 210G. The same pattern holds across classes: a D6-class crawler dozer retains 53 to 60 percent for Caterpillar against 47 to 53 percent for Komatsu, and a 3-to-5-cubic-yard wheel loader retains 54 to 60 percent for Caterpillar against 48 to 54 percent for Komatsu.

The brand gap is the most consistent finding in the data. Across classes, Caterpillar machines retain 5 to 12 percentage points more of their original list price than comparable Komatsu and Deere units at the 5,000-hour mark. EquipBook’s model-level figures tell the same story from the other direction: a Caterpillar D5 or D6XE dozer retains about 66 percent of its MSRP at three years old and about 50 percent at five years. Machines with long physical lives, a deep international second-hand market, and little technology in them hold value best — which is why the most common machines in the most common classes, the 320 and the Komatsu PC200, also generate the most transaction volume at auction.

  • Excavators: 12–15% a year. Cat 320 retains 52–58% of list price at 5,000 hours.
  • Wheel loaders: 10–13% a year. Cat 3–5 yd³ machines retain 54–60% at 5,000 hours.
  • Dozers: 10–12% a year. Cat D6-class machines retain 53–60% at 5,000 hours.
  • Five-year rule of thumb: most heavy equipment retains 40–60% of its original value at five years.

The hour thresholds

Age is the number owners watch. Hours are the number buyers pay for. A 10-year-old excavator with 4,000 hours is usually worth more than a 5-year-old machine with 9,000 hours, according to EquipBook’s hours-versus-age analysis — and the gap widens with machine type. For dozers, hours matter roughly 70 to 30 against age, because the undercarriage is more than half of a used dozer’s value and undercarriage wear is measured in hours, not birthdays. The hinge point sits near 5,000 hours: at 5,000 to 6,000 hours, a mid-size excavator holds 52 to 62 percent of its new value, and past that mark the curve steepens as buyers start pricing in undercarriage work, major component risk, and the next emissions tier.

The second threshold is the machine’s design life. Most crawler excavators are built for 8,000 to 10,000 hours of work, according to the EquipmentWatch 2026 Market Report — a figure reported second-hand here and worth treating as a planning band rather than a specification. EquipBook’s guide puts the practical advice in plainer terms: the ideal sell point for most equipment is four to six years, before major component replacements arrive, and sellers should avoid holding past the eight-to-ten-year mark, when depreciation flattens but buyer interest thins. For wheel loaders and dozers, the same analysis puts the sell-before line at 10,000 to 12,000 hours. Two things slow the slide at any age: documented maintenance history with dealer records, worth 5 to 10 percent, and excellent condition, worth 15 to 25 percent over an average unit. Service records are the cheapest residual value an owner will ever buy.

The Cat 320, year by year

Follow one machine through the curve and the abstractions turn into dollars. EquipBook puts the Caterpillar 320’s base MSRP at $265,000, and its model-year resale bands at $148,500 to $200,500 for a 2023 machine, $112,500 to $152,000 for a 2021 machine, and $50,500 to $68,500 for a 2018 machine. EquipBook labels those figures estimates based on MSRP depreciation, not observed sales, and they should be read that way — the 2023 band alone spans more than $50,000, which is the width of condition, hours, and region. A live listing offers a partial check: Proxy Equipment’s Texas listings in November 2026 showed a 2025 Cat 320 with 979 hours at $219,000, about 83 percent of MSRP after one year — a smaller first-year drop than the 20-to-30-percent rule suggests, but a single asking price on a low-hour machine, not a sale. A second illustration runs the other way: a Caterpillar 336 bought new for $450,000 in 2020 fetching $280,000 in 2025, a 62 percent residual, in MyDepreciation’s worked example — illustrative arithmetic, not a recorded transaction. The honest summary is Cleveland Brothers’ five-year finding: after five years, heavy machinery retains only 40 to 60 percent of its original value, so a $250,000 excavator is worth $100,000 to $150,000 at the five-year mark, assuming it was maintained properly and did not accumulate excessive hours.

Why the curve is bending now

The curve is not fixed. Used excavator prices have softened roughly 5 to 8 percent since their 2023 peaks, according to Ritchie Bros. auction data, and the European market in the second quarter of 2026 showed the mechanism: auction volumes rose 14 percent year on year while the median crawler excavator price rose just 1 percent, with the median age steady at eight years. More machines are reaching the market without triggering a price correction — contractors offloading fleet into a market that is absorbing it, for now. Against that softening sits a floor. New-equipment prices are rising to cover tariffs — Caterpillar expects about $2.2 billion in tariff costs for 2026 — and a machine already sitting in a yard carries no tariff in its price. That makes late-model used iron relatively more attractive with every new list-price increase, and it is the reason the steepest part of the curve, the first three years, is also where the smartest buying happens: the first owner absorbs the tariff, the financing cost, and the first-year drop, and the second owner buys the remaining 8,000 hours at a discount.

The case against the curve

Every curve deserves its skeptic, and this one has three fair objections. First, averages hide the spread: the brand gap alone runs 5 to 12 points, and a high-hour machine in a hot region can outprice a low-hour machine where work is scarce. Second, tax depreciation is not market depreciation. Construction equipment typically falls under 5- or 7-year MACRS property, and qualifying equipment acquired on or after January 19, 2025 can generally be expensed in full in year one through 100 percent bonus depreciation — which means the tax books can show a machine at zero while the auction floor still values it at half its purchase price. Confirm the treatment with an accountant; the curve describes what buyers pay, not what the IRS allows. Third, the seller’s number is not the hammer price. Ritchie Bros. charges a buyer’s fee of 10 percent up to $30,000, then 5.5 percent to $75,000, capped at $4,125 above $75,000 — on a $120,000 winning bid, that is $4,125 before transport, and the seller’s net is lower still after commission and preparation. The curve is drawn from asking prices and modelled values as much as from hammer prices, because no public dataset publishes every sale. And the curve can break: the used market spiked through 2021 and 2022, when scarcity pushed values up instead of down, and any owner who treats a historical average as a guarantee will be surprised in both directions.

What the curve means for your fleet

Sell at four to six years, before 5,000 to 6,000 hours, if you want the residual — that is where the curve is still paying you for the machine’s remaining life rather than charging you for its risk. Buy at three to five years, at the bottom of the steep part, when the first owner has absorbed the drop and the machine still has most of its working life ahead of it. Read the hour meter before the year, keep the service records, and choose machines with the deepest buyer pool — the Cat 320 and the Komatsu PC200 generate the most transaction volume at auction for a reason, and that volume is what turns a used machine back into cash. Iron does not depreciate on a schedule. It depreciates on a meter, in a market, against a backlog of new machines that keeps moving. The owners who sell well are the ones who watch all three.
