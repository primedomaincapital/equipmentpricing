What Does a New Excavator Cost in 2026?

A new excavator costs anywhere from $28,000 to more than $500,000 — and the spread between three quotes for the same machine can buy a pickup truck. The 2026 numbers by size class, and the options that move the invoice most.

8 min read

Ask three dealers for a price on the same 20-ton excavator and you will get three numbers far enough apart to buy a pickup truck with the difference. One quotes the base machine. One quotes it the way most buyers actually order it, with a cab, a coupler, and a thumb. The third quotes the machine he has on the lot, not the one in the brochure. None of them is lying. That is the first thing to understand about new excavator prices in 2026: there is no single price, and the number on the manufacturer’s website — when one exists at all — is the least informative figure in the negotiation.

Most manufacturers do not publish a price at all. Caterpillar, John Deere, Komatsu, and Volvo sell through dealers, and the dealer writes the quote. Two brands break the pattern. Kubota and Bobcat publish a suggested price for every compact excavator they sell, which makes them the only place a buyer can check a number without calling anyone. Kubota’s list starts at $28,450 for the K008-5, its smallest machine, and runs to $138,273 for the KX080-5, the largest compact in the range. Bobcat’s runs from about $32,000 for the E10 to about $141,000 for the E88. A mid-size Kubota U35-4 or Bobcat E35 — the machines most small contractors actually buy — lists at $56,000 to $58,000. Those are base-machine prices, before freight, dealer preparation, tax, and every attachment that makes the machine useful. Treat them as the floor, not the invoice.

What the market charges, by size class

Above the compact range, nobody publishes a list, so the market’s numbers come from finance guides and dealer listings that aggregate hundreds of quotes. Alliance Equipment Capital’s 2026 guide puts new prices at $30,000 to $80,000 for mini and compact machines of 1.5 to 6 tons; $80,000 to $130,000 for small machines of 6 to 10 tons; $130,000 to $200,000 for mid-size machines of 10 to 20 tons; $200,000 to $350,000 for full-size machines of 20 to 40 tons; and $350,000 to more than $500,000 for large machines of 40 to 90 tons. A second guide, from JumboBee, lands in the same territory with slightly higher ceilings: $35,000 to $95,000 for minis, $90,000 to $160,000 for compacts, $150,000 to $320,000 for medium machines of 10 to 25 tons, and $300,000 to $650,000 for large machines of 25 to 50 tons. Purple Wave, the auction house, gives the widest honest answer: about $30,000 to more than $750,000 for a new excavator, depending on size and specification.

The bands disagree at the edges by $30,000 or more, and that disagreement is itself useful information. These are market bands drawn from dealer listings and finance guides, not sticker prices, and the edges move with region, brand, and month. What they agree on is the shape: price roughly doubles with each step up in size class, and the jump from a 20-ton machine to a 35-ton machine costs more than the entire price of a mini excavator.

  • Mini / compact (1.5–6 tons): $30,000–$80,000 new. Kubota and Bobcat list prices anchor the low end.
  • Small (6–10 tons): $80,000–$130,000 new.
  • Mid-size (10–20 tons): $130,000–$200,000 new.
  • Full-size (20–40 tons): $200,000–$350,000 new.
  • Large (40–90 tons): $350,000–$500,000+ new.

The 20-ton machine, priced three ways

The Caterpillar 320 is the best-selling 20-ton excavator in the world, and it is also the best demonstration of why “the price” of an excavator is a fiction. Machinery Planet’s February 2026 pricing guide puts a new base 320 at $200,000 to $230,000, rising to $220,000 to $250,000 with Cat Grade 2D and $250,000 to $280,000 with Grade 3D. EquipBook, the valuation service, puts the base MSRP at $265,000 — $35,000 above the top of Machinery Planet’s base band. One of those numbers is describing a different machine, a different month, or a different definition of “base.” EquipBook’s figure is a modelled MSRP; Machinery Planet’s is a dealer-derived band. Neither is a transaction price.

A live listing settles the argument partway. Proxy Equipment’s Texas listings in November 2026 showed a 2025 Caterpillar 320 with 979 hours at $219,000 — a one-year-old machine, not a new one, but a real asking price rather than a modelled one, and it sits inside Machinery Planet’s base band rather than near EquipBook’s MSRP. Komatsu’s rival, the PC210, lands near $215,000 new, and the 320 itself near $238,000 once standard options are included, according to a 2026 buyer’s guide from Jiuhong Machinery — a secondary source, read with that caveat, but consistent with the dealer bands above it. Step up a class and the spread widens further: dealer listings for the Caterpillar 336, the 36-ton machine, show a Toromont Cat asking price of $410,000 in Ontario and a Finning Cat asking price of $555,900 in Edmonton — different specifications, different regions, both asking prices rather than sales. Averaging them would produce a number no dealer would recognize.

Where the invoice actually grows

The base machine is the cheapest version of the excavator anyone sells, and almost nobody buys it. Grade control is the single largest step: on the 320, moving from base to Grade 3D adds roughly $30,000 to $50,000, according to Machinery Planet’s bands — more than the price of a small mini excavator. A cab instead of a canopy, a long stick, extra counterweight, and an extended warranty each add their own line. Then come the attachments, whose purchase prices are rarely published at all. The rental market shows the scale: in Houston in 2026, a hydraulic thumb rents for $90 to $160 a day, a quick coupler for $35 to $75 a day, and a hydraulic breaker for $250 to $450 a day, according to CountBricks’ rental guides. Run a breaker for a 20-day month and the rental bill alone is $5,000 to $9,000 — more than the monthly payment on a small mini excavator, which Alliance Equipment Capital puts at $290 to $1,550 a month on 60-month terms. Buy the attachments outright and they are capital items in their own right, not accessories.

None of the list prices cited here include freight, dealer preparation, or sales tax. A machine shipped across the country and delivered to a job site can carry thousands of dollars in transport before it digs its first bucket, and the tax treatment varies by state. The out-the-door number is the only number that matters, and it is the number no manufacturer publishes.

The tariff line in every 2026 quote

There is one more line in the 2026 quote that did not exist two years ago. Caterpillar expects about $2.2 billion in tariff costs for full-year 2026, with roughly $600 million landing in the third quarter alone, and realized $663 million in price increases in that quarter and $1.68 billion across the first nine months. The arithmetic is blunt: list prices are rising to cover tariffs, and buyers are paying them because the order backlog leaves little alternative. A machine already sitting in a yard carries no tariff in its price — which is why the used market, covered in our earlier articles, has become the release valve for buyers who cannot wait for a new delivery slot.

The case for not believing any of these numbers

Every figure in this article deserves a skeptical reading, and the honest version of that skepticism runs as follows. List prices are not transaction prices: dealers discount when the backlog eases and hold firm when it does not, and the spread between regions is wide — this site’s own thesis notes the same excavator changing hands for $180,000 in Texas and $240,000 in Ohio. Used machines sell for 20 to 40 percent below new, according to Purple Wave, and used values have softened roughly 5 to 8 percent since their 2023 peaks, according to Ritchie Bros. auction data — so the gap between a new quote and a late-model used machine is narrower than it was two years ago, and the used machine is getting cheaper while the new one gets more expensive. Financing changes the math more than a $10,000 price difference does: rates in 2026 typically run 6.5 to 11 percent APR on 60-month terms for creditworthy borrowers, according to Alliance Equipment Capital, and the Section 179 deduction limit for 2026 is $2,560,000 — enough for most buyers to deduct the full purchase price in the year of acquisition, subject to their accountant’s advice. A buyer who compares monthly payments instead of sticker prices is comparing the right numbers.

What to do with a range, not a price

Buy the quote, not the brochure. Get three written out-the-door quotes on the same specification sheet — same model, same cab, same grade-control package, same attachments — and make the dealers price the options separately, so the $30,000 grade-control step and the attachment package are visible instead of buried. Price the late-model used alternative alongside the new quote, using the auction and dealer benchmarks in our earlier articles, and remember that the first-year depreciation on a new machine is the largest single cost of ownership. And treat the $200,000-to-$280,000 spread on the Cat 320 as the lesson, not the problem: in a market with no published price, the negotiation is the price. The excavator market will not give you a single number. It will give you a range, a spec sheet, and a dealer who knows which of the two you read first.
