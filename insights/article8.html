Equipment Financing vs. Cash: What the Interest Rate Does to the Total Cost

A $300,000 excavator financed at 7.4% costs about $60,000 in interest over five years — and generates $90,000 to $105,000 in year-one tax savings. The interest rate is not the decision. The tax bracket is.

7 min read

A contractor in Texas needs a $300,000 excavator. The dealer offers two ways to pay for it: $300,000 in cash, or a loan at 7.4% over five years. The cash price is lower — obviously, the loan costs about $60,000 in interest. But the contractor is in the 35% tax bracket, and under the 100% bonus depreciation made permanent by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (P.L. 119-21), the full $300,000 is deductible in the year the machine is placed in service, provided it was acquired after January 19, 2025. The year-one tax saving is $105,000. The interest on the whole five-year loan is about $60,000. The loan does not cost $60,000. After the tax saving, it costs less than the cash — by about $45,000. That is the arithmetic that decides whether to finance or pay cash, and it has almost nothing to do with the interest rate.

The tax shield is the rate that matters

The starting point is the deduction. Section 179 of the Internal Revenue Code lets a business expense the cost of qualifying equipment in the year it is bought, up to a limit of $2,560,000 for 2026, with the phase-out beginning at $4,090,000 of total equipment purchases, under IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32. For most contractors, that limit is not binding — a $300,000 excavator is well inside it. And for purchases above the limit, 100% bonus depreciation applies to the remainder, permanently, for property acquired after January 19, 2025. The two provisions together mean that a business with taxable income can deduct the full cost of a new excavator in the year it buys it.

The value of that deduction depends entirely on the buyer's tax rate. At a combined federal and state rate of 30%, a $300,000 machine produces $90,000 of year-one tax savings. At 35%, it produces $105,000. That is the number to compare with the interest cost, not the sticker price. A buyer in the 35% bracket who pays cash spends $300,000 and gets $105,000 back at tax time — a net cost of $195,000. A buyer who finances spends $300,000 plus about $60,000 of interest over five years, and gets the same $105,000 back — a net cost of $255,000, spread over five years instead of paid on day one. The cash buyer saves $60,000 in total. The financed buyer keeps $300,000 in the bank on day one and pays it back over five years, at a cost of $60,000, while the tax saving arrives in year one. Which is better depends on what the $300,000 earns while it is still in the bank.

The rate bands, priced out

The interest cost depends on where the buyer borrows. Bank loans for heavy equipment run 4% to 8% for established borrowers. Dealer financing runs 3.5% to 10%, with promotional rates as low as 0% for up to 48 months — Volvo has offered 0% on qualified machines for four years. Online lenders run 9% to 20%. The Equipment Leasing and Finance Association's average yield on equipment finance was 7.4%, and strong-credit borrowers are quoted 6.5% to 8.5% APR on terms up to 84 months.

Run the $300,000 machine through those bands on a 60-month term. At 6.5%, the monthly payment is about $5,871 and the total interest is about $52,000. At 7.4% — the ELFA average — the payment is about $5,997 and the interest is about $60,000. At 8.5%, the payment is about $6,156 and the interest is about $69,000. In every case in the strong-credit band, the five-year interest cost is less than the year-one tax saving at a 30% rate ($90,000), and well below it at 35% ($105,000). The financing pays for itself in tax terms before the second year begins — provided the buyer has the taxable income to use the deduction.

The online-lender band is where the arithmetic flips. At 15% over 60 months, the monthly payment on $300,000 is about $7,137 and the total interest is about $128,000 — more than the year-one tax saving at either rate. At 20%, the payment is about $7,982 and the interest is about $179,000. A buyer who borrows at 15% or 20% is paying more in interest than the tax code gives back, and the cash buyer wins by a wide margin. The break-even sits near 10% to 11% for a buyer in the 30% bracket on a five-year term: below it, financing wins on the tax math; above it, cash wins. The rate is not a detail. It is the decision.

The lease alternative, and the used-machine discount

Leasing changes the comparison again. A $1 buyout lease — the most popular structure in the industry — lets the buyer use the machine for a fixed monthly payment and own it for $1 at the end of the term, and lease payments typically run 20% to 30% lower than loan payments on the same machine, because the lessor retains the residual value and the tax treatment differs. For a buyer who wants the lowest monthly cash outflow, the lease wins. For a buyer who wants the deduction, the loan usually wins, because the buyer owns the asset and claims the depreciation. The choice is between cash flow and tax, and the right answer depends on which one the business is short of.

The used machine changes the numbers in the other direction. A used excavator runs $15,000 to $320,000 — 40% to 60% less than a new machine of the same class — and the same Section 179 and bonus depreciation rules apply to used equipment, provided it is new to the buyer. A $150,000 used excavator in the 35% bracket produces $52,500 of year-one tax savings, against about $30,000 of interest on a five-year loan at 7.4%. The tax shield is smaller in dollars but the same in proportion, and the cash required is half. For a buyer whose constraint is cash rather than tax, the used machine financed at a strong-credit rate is the cheapest way into the work.

The counterargument: cash is still king when the income is not there

The case for cash deserves its weight, because the tax argument has a hole in it: the deduction is only worth something if the buyer has taxable income to offset. A contractor in a loss year, a startup with no profits yet, or a business that has already used its Section 179 limit on other purchases gets no year-one saving from the deduction — and without the saving, the loan costs the full $60,000 of interest, and the cash buyer wins by exactly that amount. The same is true for a buyer in a low bracket: at a 12% rate, the $300,000 machine produces only $36,000 of tax savings, against $60,000 of interest, and cash wins again. The financing advantage is not universal. It belongs to profitable businesses in high brackets, and it disappears the moment the income does.

There is also the risk argument. A financed machine is a fixed monthly obligation — about $6,000 a month on the $300,000 example — that must be paid whether the machine is working or not. A cash machine has no payment. In a downturn, the contractor with the paid-for fleet survives; the contractor with five financed machines and no work does not. The interest rate is not the only cost of borrowing. The obligation is a cost too, and it is the one that kills businesses in a recession.

For a buyer, the practical conclusion is to run three numbers before deciding: the year-one tax saving at your actual rate, the total interest over the term at the rate you can actually borrow at, and the monthly payment against your worst month of revenue. If the tax saving exceeds the interest and the payment is comfortable, finance it and keep the cash. If the income is not there, or the rate is above 10%, or the payment would strain a slow month, pay cash — or buy used. And for anyone in the business of equipment pricing, the missing number is now obvious. Nobody publishes the break-even rate by tax bracket, by term, by machine class. The firm that does will own the next argument in equipment finance.
