The Data Center Machine: How Power Demand Rewrote the Equipment Order Book

On October 29, 2026, Caterpillar announced the largest quarter in its hundred-year history — $20.7 billion in sales and a $78 billion backlog. The machines are selling faster than ever, and the reason has almost nothing to do with construction sites.

6 min read

On October 29, 2026, Caterpillar's chief executive Joe Creed stood in front of investors and announced the largest quarter in the company's hundred-year history: $20.7 billion in sales, up 17 percent on a year earlier, and an order backlog of $78 billion, nearly double the year before. The stock fell anyway. That contradiction is the whole story of heavy equipment pricing right now. The machines are selling faster than ever, and the reason they are selling has almost nothing to do with the construction sites they were built for.

The power segment ate the company

Caterpillar's third-quarter numbers split the business into two different companies wearing one badge. Power & Energy, the segment that builds engines, turbines, and generators, posted $8.8 billion in sales, up 18 percent, with power generation sales up 30 percent and dealer sales of power-generation equipment up 75 percent. Orders for large reciprocating engines now stretch into 2029, and turbine orders into 2030. Construction Industries, the segment that sells excavators, dozers, and loaders to contractors, also grew — about $8 billion in sales, up 18 percent, with sales to users up 20 percent for a seventh straight quarter. Resource Industries, serving mining and rail, added $4.6 billion, up 15 percent. Operating profit margin hit 20.4 percent, against 17.3 percent a year earlier, and profit per share reached $7.13, up from $4.88.

Read the mix, not the headline. Power generation sales have gone from $4.29 billion in 2021 to $10.28 billion in 2025, and $9.34 billion in the first nine months of 2026 alone. The data-center build-out is now the single largest driver of demand for the world's biggest equipment maker. A contractor pricing a 2027 fleet plan is competing for factory slots against hyperscalers buying backup power by the megawatt — and the hyperscalers are not price-sensitive.

Tariffs are now a line item in every quote

The second force moving prices is the tariff bill. Caterpillar expects about $2.2 billion in tariff costs for full-year 2026, with roughly $600 million landing in the third quarter alone. Against that, the company realized $663 million in price increases in the quarter and $1.68 billion across the first nine months. The arithmetic is blunt: list prices are rising to cover tariffs, and buyers are paying them because the backlog leaves no alternative. Construction Industries carried about half the third-quarter tariff cost. When a dealer quotes a new excavator today, the number embeds a trade-policy cost that did not exist two years ago, and there is no sign of it coming back out.

That pass-through is what makes the used market so interesting. A machine already sitting in a yard has no tariff in its price. With new-equipment lead times stretching and list prices climbing, late-model used iron has become the release valve — the same dynamic that pushed used values up through 2021 and 2022, now driven by a different shortage. Rental fleets feel it too: large excavators rent for roughly $8,500 to $12,000 a month, and every month of delay on a new delivery is a month a contractor pays rent instead of building equity.

The counterargument: the dirt business is cooling

None of this means construction demand is booming. Look past the segment totals. Construction Industries grew 18 percent in the quarter after growing nearly 35 percent in the second quarter — a sharp deceleration. Caterpillar expects a larger-than-usual seasonal drawdown of dealer inventory to cut about $1 billion from Construction Industries sales in the fourth quarter. And in Resource Industries, where reported sales rose 15 percent, deliveries to actual users rose just 4 percent. Mining, the segment's core market, is barely moving. The record quarter is a power story with a construction kicker, not a construction boom, and a buyer who reads it as the latter will overpay for iron the market may not need next year.

The share price said as much. Despite the record sales, Caterpillar's stock dropped on results day and sat more than 40 percent below its 52-week high by the end of October. Investors were not disputing the quarter. They were asking what the company is when its growth engine is selling generators to data centers rather than dozers to contractors — and whether that growth survives the first serious pause in AI infrastructure spending.

What this means for equipment prices

Three implications follow for anyone buying, selling, or renting iron in 2027. First, new-equipment prices will stay sticky upward: a $78 billion backlog and order books running to 2029 give manufacturers no reason to discount, and tariffs give them a reason to raise prices again. Second, the used market is where the pricing action is — scarcity of new machines plus tariff-free used values makes late-model equipment the rational buy for contractors who can inspect it properly. Third, the rental decision gets easier, not harder: with utilization uncertain and technology moving fast, paying $9,500 a month for a machine you can return beats financing $290,000 of depreciating steel whose resale value depends on a data-center cycle you cannot forecast.

The equipment market spent a century tracking housing starts and highway bills. It now tracks power demand and trade policy. The contractors who price their fleets against the old cycle will keep being surprised by the new one — and the surprise, as Caterpillar's quarter showed, cuts both ways.
