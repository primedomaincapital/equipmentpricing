Equipment Auction Prices vs. Dealer Prices: The Spread

In February 2025, a 2021 Caterpillar 320 with 2,800 hours sold at a Ritchie Bros. auction in Las Vegas for $178,000. Three weeks later, a nearly identical machine — same year, 3,100 hours — was listed by a Cat dealer in Phoenix at $219,000. It sold within a month.

5 min read

Same machine. Same market. $41,000 apart. This spread — between what equipment fetches at auction and what dealers charge — is the most important inefficiency in the used equipment market. Understanding it is worth real money.

Why the Spread Exists

The auction-dealer spread isn't a mystery. It's the price of four things dealers provide that auctions don't:

1. Inspection and reconditioning. A reputable dealer inspects, services, and often reconditions before listing. Fresh fluids, filters, minor repairs, detailing. This costs $3,000–$8,000 on a typical machine — real value, but nowhere near the full spread.

2. Warranty. Many dealers offer 30–90 day powertrain warranties on used machines. This has genuine value — a powertrain failure in the first month is the buyer's nightmare scenario, and the warranty eliminates it. Actuarially, this is worth $2,000–$5,000.

3. Financing. Dealers arrange financing; auctions generally don't (though this is changing with online platforms). For buyers who need to finance, the dealer's financing access has practical value beyond the rate itself.

4. Recourse. If something is materially wrong, you can go back to the dealer. At auction, the hammer falls and the machine is yours — "as-is, where-is," with no one to call. This risk differential is the largest component of the spread, and the hardest to quantify.

Add it up: $3,000–$8,000 (reconditioning) + $2,000–$5,000 (warranty value) + financing access + recourse. The rational spread should be $10,000–$20,000 on a $200,000 machine. The actual spread is often $30,000–$50,000. The difference is dealer margin, overhead, and — frankly — buyer willingness to pay for comfort.

The Spread by Machine Type

The spread isn't uniform. It varies predictably:

High spread (25–35%): Dozers, motor graders, specialized machines. Fewer buyers, less price transparency, higher dealer margins. A used D6 might show a $60,000 spread on a $200,000 machine.

Medium spread (15–25%): Excavators, wheel loaders. Liquid markets with good auction volume. The spread reflects real services but includes healthy dealer margin.

Low spread (10–15%): Skid steers, mini excavators, compact equipment. High auction volume, many buyers, efficient price discovery. Dealers compete more aggressively here.

Minimal spread (<10%): Late-model, low-hour machines from major brands. When the machine is essentially new, the auction-dealer distinction blurs — everyone's pricing off the same narrow band.

How to Exploit the Spread

Buy at auction, inspect like a dealer. The spread is widest for buyers who can replicate the dealer's risk-reduction themselves. If you can inspect thoroughly (or hire an independent inspector for $500–$1,000), arrange your own financing, and accept the no-recourse reality, you capture most of the spread as savings.

The inspection-day strategy. Ritchie Bros. and other major auction houses offer pre-auction inspection periods. Serious buyers spend the day before the auction going through every machine on their list. The buyers who do this consistently outperform those who bid from photos. Every time.

Know the reserve game. Many auction machines have undisclosed reserves. If bidding stalls below reserve, the machine doesn't sell — but the auction house will often facilitate a post-auction negotiation. Some of the best auction deals happen after the hammer doesn't fall.

Dealer trade-ins. When a dealer takes a machine on trade toward new, they need to move it. Trade-ins are often priced more aggressively than retail-used inventory because the dealer's margin is in the new sale, not the used disposal. Ask dealers specifically about recent trade-ins.

End-of-quarter timing. Dealers have sales targets. The last two weeks of each quarter — especially Q4 — produce the most negotiable dealer pricing. The spread compresses when dealers need to move iron.

When to Pay the Dealer Premium

The spread isn't always worth capturing. Pay dealer prices when:

  • It's your first machine in a category (you need the recourse)
  • The machine is critical-path (downtime costs exceed the spread)
  • You lack inspection expertise (the warranty is worth more to you)
  • Financing is the constraint (dealer-arranged financing unlocks the purchase)
"The auction price tells you what the machine is worth. The dealer price tells you what peace of mind costs. Both are legitimate. Just know which one you're buying."

The Bottom Line

The auction-dealer spread is persistent because it reflects real differences in risk, service, and convenience. But it's consistently wider than those differences justify — which means informed buyers who can manage their own risk capture significant savings. In a $200,000 purchase, the knowledge of this spread alone is worth $20,000–$30,000. That's an expensive thing not to know.
